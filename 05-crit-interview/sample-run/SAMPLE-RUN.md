# Sample run: a cleaning company owner and a discount request

Every person and business here is invented.

Rosa Delgado runs a cleaning company with three vans and eleven staff. Most of her work is homes. Her one big office account, a property manager with four buildings, just asked for 15 percent off in exchange for a two-year contract. She has until Friday to answer.

**What Rosa typed:**

```
/crit my biggest commercial client wants a discount
```

---

## Step 1: Context

**Claude:** Tell me what is going on the way you would tell a smart friend over coffee: what happened, who is involved, what you have already tried, what is already decided, and what you are worried about. If there is a real document, like their email or your numbers for the account, paste it in.

**Rosa:** *(pastes the property manager's email, then writes)* We've cleaned their four buildings for three years. It's about a quarter of our revenue. Their new facilities manager, Dana, started in the summer and she's going through every vendor. She says another company quoted lower. I don't know if that's true. My margins on that account are already thinner than on homes because of the night shifts. I'm not going to drop them, that's decided. I'm scared that if I say no they walk, and if I say yes I'm working for almost nothing for two years.

**Claude:** Here is what I am working from:
- One account, four buildings, three years, about a quarter of your revenue.
- A new facilities manager is reviewing every vendor and says she has a lower quote. Unconfirmed.
- She is offering a two-year contract in exchange for 15 percent off.
- Your margin on this account is already thinner than on homes, because of night shifts.
- Decided: you are keeping the account if you can. Deadline Friday.

What did I get wrong or leave out?

**Rosa:** That's right. Also my lead cleaner on that account, Marcus, is the reason they've never complained.

## Step 2: Role

**Claude:** I'd like to take this on as a pricing strategist who spent fifteen years in commercial cleaning and facilities work, and who used to sit on the buying side as a procurement lead. That lens fits because Dana is doing what new buyers do in their first months, and someone who has done her job knows what she actually needs to bring back to her boss. Want me to use this role, or would a different expert see this better?

**Rosa:** Use that one.

## Step 3: Interview

**Claude:** I'm going to ask you a few questions before I tell you anything. One at a time.

First: when Dana took over, what has she complained about, if anything, beyond price?

**Rosa:** Nothing really. She asked for a monthly report on what we did in each building. We never had to do that before. I sent one in September and haven't since.

**Claude:** If you gave her nothing off the price, what is the most you could give her in service that costs you little but she would value?

**Rosa:** Honestly, the reports. And we could do the lobby glass every week instead of every two weeks, that's maybe an hour a building. Marcus could do a walkthrough with her once a quarter.

**Claude:** Last one. If you lost this account in March, what would you do with those night-shift hours and the people on them?

**Rosa:** Two of them would move to homes, I have a waiting list. The other two I'd probably lose. It would hurt for about three months.

**Claude:** That's enough for me to work with.

## Step 4: Task

**Claude:** Task: three ways to answer Dana's discount request, ranked, with the reply email for the one you pick, for you to send before Friday.

Is that the job?

**Rosa:** Yes.

**Claude:** Two ways to finish. A) I do it now, here, as this role. B) I hand you a ready prompt you can paste into any AI or save for later.

**Rosa:** A.

## Step 5: Deliver

**The read.** A new facilities manager asking every vendor for a discount usually needs to show her boss she reviewed the contracts, not necessarily that she cut every one by 15 percent. The monthly report she asked for and you stopped sending is the real opening. You have also learned something useful: losing the account would hurt for about three months, not forever. That is a stronger position than it felt like when her email arrived.

**Three moves, ranked**

1. **Trade the term, not the price.** Offer 4 percent off for the two-year contract, plus the monthly building report, weekly lobby glass, and a quarterly walkthrough with Marcus. She gets a number to show her boss and three things she can point to. You give up far less than 15 percent, and the extra work is roughly four hours a month.
2. **Ask to see the lower quote's scope, not its price.** Lower quotes for four buildings often leave out nights, glass, or supplies. A one-line ask ("Happy to look at this together. Can you share what the other quote covers?") is polite, and it tells you whether the threat is real.
3. **Make Marcus visible.** Dana has never met the reason the buildings run well. Put his name on the monthly report and on the walkthrough. This is the move she would not expect, and it is the one that makes switching vendors feel risky to her.

**Biggest risk:** if the other quote is real and covers the same scope, 4 percent may not be enough. Decide before you send what your walk-away number is. Based on your answer about March, that number could be lower than you think.

**The reply email (move 1, with move 2 folded in)**

```
Subject: Two-year agreement for the four buildings

Dana,

Thank you for laying out what you need from vendors. Here is what we can do for a two-year agreement:

- 4 percent off the current rate, starting January
- A monthly report for each building, by the 5th of the month
- Lobby glass weekly instead of every two weeks
- A quarterly walkthrough with Marcus, who leads your night crew

If the other quote covers something we don't, send me its scope and I'll look at it with you.

Rosa
```

**Claude:** What do you like? What don't you like? What would you change first?

**Rosa:** I like the Marcus part. I don't like leading with a number. Can the price go last?

**Claude:** *(moves the 4 percent line to the end of the list and adds "in return for the two-year term")*

---

Nothing was sent. Rosa read the draft twice, decided her walk-away number on paper, and sent the email herself on Thursday morning.
